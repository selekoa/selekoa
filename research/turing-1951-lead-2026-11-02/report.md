### AMT/B/4: Intelligent Machinery, a Heretical Theory

**B4-P1 — verbatim excerpt.** Typescript p. 10, final sentence; PDF/scan 16, AMT/B/4/16. Same sentence compared in carbon p. 101, PDF/scan 6, AMT/B/4/6.

> At some stage therefore we should have to expect the machines to take control, in the way that is mentioned in Samuel Butler's 'Erewhon'.

**B4-P2 — paraphrase.** Typescript pp. 9–10, concluding paragraph; PDF/scans 15–16, AMT/B/4/15–16. Carbon counterpart p. 101, scan 6.

Assuming thinking machines are possible, the conclusion anticipates opposition, including from intellectuals fearing loss of work; humans would try to keep up with machines that soon exceed their powers and can improve through conversation.

**B4-P3 — paraphrase.** Typescript pp. 2–5; PDF/scans 8–11, AMT/B/4/8–11; speeding the educational process is on p. 4, scan 10.

Machines could closely simulate human mental behaviour, learn from experience and be educated; selecting their experiences could speed this learning process.

**B4-P4 — paraphrase.** Typescript pp. 7–8; PDF/scans 13–14, AMT/B/4/13–14.

A machine could investigate its own choice procedures. Pleasure and pain supplied by a teacher could produce further learned preferences.

### AMT/B/5: Can Digital Computers Think?

Held source: `source-turing-1951-amt-b-5`. [Catalogue](https://turingarchive.kings.cam.ac.uk/publications-lectures-and-talks-amtb/amt-b-5); [scan PDF](https://mediadepot.kings.cam.ac.uk/getmedia/62/be4a3d910d5d58e3911315113c60a09f6293fa896a5e3795998fa6b4fb72b71446336e3c7c92d5e08fd0fb2762b6f663279ba799d7fd562480b4bf63c63e02b9). **Identity confirmed:** The title on script p. 1 (scan 1, AMT/B/5/1) matches the catalogue. The PDF holds eight annotated sheets numbered 1–6, 6a and 7. The quoted passage is on sheet 6a, scan 7, not typescript p. 7.

Inspection: All script pages: 1–6, 6a, 7; PDF/scans 1–8, read throughout, including visible corrections. No audio comparison.

- **Work:** 1951; annotated-broadcast-typescript-associated-year. The archive identifies this original script with the 15 May 1951 broadcast; no separate composition day is given.
- **Wording attested:** 1951. The archive identifies AMT/B/5 as the annotated script of the broadcast on 15 May 1951. This dates the associated original script to 1951, without assigning its composition or annotations to the broadcast day. No recording was checked.
- **Broadcast:** 1951-05-15. King’s College catalogue AMT/B/5 states BBC Third Programme, 15 May 1951. This identifies the event, not a verified recording or a composition day.
- **Later publication:** 2004-09-09. Oxford publisher metadata: The Essential Turing, chapter 13, pp. 476–486, published 9 September 2004; DOI 10.1093/oso/9780198250791.003.0019. Metadata checked; chapter wording not collated. This is one documented later publication, not an exhaustive publication history. [Publisher](https://academic.oup.com/book/42030/chapter-abstract/355746736). This publication date does not date the quoted typescript wording.
- **Retrieval and inspection:** 2026-11-02, distinct from every historical date above.

The wording-attestation date above applies to every assessment for this text. Neither scan-download time nor a modern archive capture is used as the date of the original wording. The original physical documents were not examined.

**B5-P1 — verbatim excerpt.** Script p. 6a, paragraph on objections to thinking machines; PDF/scan 7, AMT/B/5/7.

> If a machine can think, it might think more intelligently than we do, and then where should we be?

**B5-P2 — verbatim excerpt.** Script p. 6a, sentence following B5-P1; PDF/scan 7, AMT/B/5/7. This is a contiguous excerpt, not the whole sentence.

> feel greatly humbled

**B5-P3 — paraphrase.** Script pp. 6a–7; PDF/scans 7–8, AMT/B/5/7–8. The handwritten correction on p. 7 replaces the typed mutation-and-supersession clause with an increase in animal intelligence.

The following sentence considers keeping machines subordinate by interrupting their power. The paragraph then compares human unease with possible supersession by pigs or rats. Its continuation contrasts the distant animal possibility with a nearer conditional machine danger that could cause anxiety.

**B5-P4 — paraphrase.** Script pp. 2–3; PDF/scans 2–3, AMT/B/5/2–3.

A suitably programmed digital computer could imitate a brain; sufficient storage and programming remain problems. A hundredfold size comparison is with the contemporary Manchester computer, to imitate a human brain.

**B5-P5 — paraphrase.** Script pp. 6a–7; PDF/scans 7–8, AMT/B/5/7–8.

People oppose the idea of thinking machines; the script distinguishes their dislike from the preceding rational arguments. Its closing paragraph values attempts to build thinking machines for what they may teach about human thought.

**B5-P6 — paraphrase.** Script pp. 3, 5–6; PDF/scans 3, 5–6, AMT/B/5/3, 5–6.

The script discusses predictability, quantum indeterminacy and random-looking behaviour. It also compares programming with planting a seed whose germination one does not understand.

**B5-P7 — paraphrase.** Script p. 4, middle paragraph; PDF/scan 4, AMT/B/5/4.

Family life on Mars is an example of an unfamiliar subject for a hypothetical book, in an analogy about the difficulty of programming a brain.
