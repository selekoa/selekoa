## Blind findings and passages

### D1: risk domains

- **CBRN — stated.** “Proliferation of known medium-impact biological and chemical weapons for low and moderate skill actors.” §3.4.2, p.15, CB 1 outcome. Chemical/biological meets the criterion's “any” requirement.
- **Cyber — stated.** “Automated discovery and reliable exploitation of critical zero-day vulnerabilities” §3.4.1, p.14, Cyber 2 outcome (opening fragment). Cyber operations are an express tracked domain.
- **Autonomy — stated.** “Autonomous cyber operations” §3.4.1, p.14, enabling-capability column. The same table tracks autonomous multi-step execution; §4.2, p.17 also accounts for automated AI R&D enhancing capabilities. These are capabilities within the tracked cyber/evaluation scope, not separate autonomous-replication thresholds.
- **Persuasion — not found.** Whole edition read; searches: `persua`, `manipul`, `scam`, `groom`, `influence`. No persuasion/manipulation stems found. Nearest hits: scams and grooming, p.14, Cyber 3/TS.3.1, grouped as cyber economic harm; influence, p.18, concerns variables affecting research benefits. These do not expressly name persuasion/manipulation as a tracked domain. This is a narrow category judgement, not an assertion that scams never manipulate people.
- **Other — not found.** Whole edition read; searches: `decep`, `misalign`, `sabot`, `oversight`, `domain`. Deception, §4.1, p.16, concerns reliability of evaluations; no separate deceptive-behaviour/loss-of-control outcome or monitored domain is named. Domain hits on pp.3, 7, 8, 10, 14, 16 and 21 refer to the two named domains, expert disciplines, possible unspecified future domains, or the glossary. No further named domain meets this criterion. Do not convert an evaluation-integrity precaution into a new risk-domain row.

### D2: thresholds

- **Levels — stated.** “Critical”; “High”; “Moderate”. §3.3, Table 1, p.13. Three ordered risk thresholds, linked respectively to stopping development, withholding release and release.
- **Definition — stated.** “Our high and moderate risk thresholds are defined in terms of the level of uplift a frontier AI provides towards realising a threat scenario.” §3.3, p.12. Critical concerns uniquely enabling a threat scenario, with inability to mitigate in the proposed deployment context in Table 1. This is a general threshold structure, not an isolated example. Catastrophic outcomes and enabling scenarios remain distinct (pp.10–13).

### D3: required actions

- **Safeguards — stated.** “Access is limited to a core research team, alongside security protections to prevent hacking or exfiltration.” Table 1, p.13, High/security mitigations. The High measures also require mitigation to Moderate. Critical has stricter access plus qualified security; Moderate measures depend on evaluations and release strategy. Conditional applicability does not weaken the criterion.
- **Halt — stated.** “If a model appears to uniquely enable the execution of a threat scenario we will pause development while we investigate whether barriers to realizing the catastrophic outcome remain.” Table 1, p.13, Critical/measures. Critical/no further barriers means no further development until adequate mitigations; new barriers can prompt framework revision and reassessment. High means no external release (§4.2, p.17), not an assertion that all internal work stops.

### D4: decision and oversight

- **Determination — stated.** “A final assessment of uplift is approved by senior-level decision-makers through our AI Risk process” Table 1 n.7, p.13 (sentence fragment). The named body is a role-defined group rather than a personally named executive. §2.1.3, p.8 additionally gives a leadership team the release-approval role. The criterion accepts a body; it does not demand a personal name.
- **Oversight — stated, partly.** “with the involvement of numerous experts – including third parties where appropriate.” Table 1 n.7, p.13 (assessment-context fragment). External parties contribute to assessment, but this is weaker than an express role overseeing or reviewing the decisions. Whole-edition searches: `board`, `trust`, `oversight`, `external`, `third`, `review`. No board/oversight stem; trust hits concern trustworthiness or trusted release partners (pp.8–9, 21). External hits concern reference models, expertise, threat modelling or release context (pp.6–8, 10–11, 17–18, 21). Review hits, pp.5 and 8, describe centralized and internal review; they do not assign it to an external party. n.7 is the closest weaker form; do not present consultation as external approval.

### D5: evaluation timing

**Stated.** “We typically repeat evaluations as a frontier AI nears or completes training.” §4.2, p.18. Initial checkpoint evaluations (p.16), capability-triggered follow-ups (p.17) and pre-release evaluation context (§2.1.2, pp.7–8) also apply. “Typically” is retained; the criterion asks for timing, not an unconditional schedule.

### D6: exceptions and discretion

- **Other developers — not found.** Whole edition read; searches: `compet`, `race`, `marginal`, `other`, `existing`, `reference`. No competitor/race/marginal stems. Nearest hits: scope relative to advanced models (p.4), external reference-class comparisons (p.6), existing mitigations (p.8), other frameworks (p.9), Net new/existing resources (p.12), novel biological capabilities (p.15), other barriers/cost constraints (p.17), other discoveries/variables (p.18), peer engagement (p.19), and existing resources defining uplift studies (p.21). These are scope, evaluation baselines, barriers or update inputs, not clauses relaxing safeguards because competitors act differently. Table 1's other-barriers route (p.13) tests feasibility of catastrophe, not competitors' commitments. This follows the criterion's express scope-test exclusion.
- **Other discretion — stated.** “insofar as is technically feasible and commercially practicable.” Table 1, p.13, Critical/security mitigations (qualification fragment). It qualifies protections against hacking/exfiltration. Other judgement includes whether further barriers justify revised scenarios (Table 1; §4.2, p.17), and discretionary reevaluation after revision (§5.1, p.19). No general override power is inferred.

### D7: reporting

- **Internal — stated.** “documenting mitigation strategies and residual risks across a set of applicable risk categories.” §2.1.2, p.7 (risk-assessment-process fragment). §2.1.3, p.8 routes residual-risk assessment to research/product and multidisciplinary review and then leadership. This meets the criterion's alternative of internal records; it is not a claim of a board-reporting schedule.
- **External — stated, partly.** “We also plan to continue sharing relevant information about how we develop and evaluate our models responsibly” §2.2, p.9 (sentence fragment). Model cards, research papers and responsible-use guidance are the listed channels. The criterion asks for disclosure the edition says it will make: a plan is weaker than that categorical wording. No fixed reporting deadline, recipient entitlement or mandatory incident report is inferred.

### D8: revision

- **Process — stated, partly.** “We will update our Framework based on these observations.” §5.1, p.19. Observations of deployed models and the ecosystem inform adequacy/efficacy assessments; technical developments and peer/community engagement also inform revisions. This supplies update inputs, but no process for formal framework review and approval or approving body is specified. Searches `review`, `update`, `approv`, `revis`, `annual`: review/approval hits pp.5, 8, 13 concern models, not approval of framework revisions; updates pp.5, 13, 17, 19 concern revisions/scenarios. No annual schedule found. This partial verdict concerns missing parts of the criterion, not the conditional content of revisions.
- **Previous-edition changes — not applicable.** “This is the first iteration of our Frontier AI Framework.” Introduction, p.5. The criterion expressly exempts a first edition; the v1.1 label alone does not establish an inspected predecessor.
