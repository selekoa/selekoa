### Descartes, Discours de la méthode (1637)

`descartes-1637-discours` · Book · 1637 (publication). Year in the title-page imprint (Leiden, Ian Maire), read on the page image.

- **C1 — bears on** (`pre-c1-descartes-1637-context`; context; register nonfiction; 1637 (publication))
  - Descartes's Discours de la méthode (1637), Part V, says that a machine could be made to utter words, even words suited to actions on its body, but not to arrange them to answer the sense of whatever is said in its presence, and that a machine doing some things as well as people would fail in others; by these two means machines could always be told from people.

That some performances would not show knowledge is, on this reading, said of performances, not of evidence from a test.
  - **Maintainer decision 5a (2026-11-04):** Descartes (1637) is context on C1; Jefferson (1949) heads C1. The checker's narrower reading b is noted.
  - Qualification: Context under decision 5a. The cross-family check read the passage as meeting C1 for restricted probes (a machine's words in response, or some tasks done as well as people, would not show knowledge) and would have it head C1 (reading b, held in both its reports); it called reading a a restriction beyond C1's present wording, to be stated in C1 if intended. The admission plan proposes that scope note. Spelling as printed, long s as s.
  - Part V, p. 57 (djvu page 61), the first means (context): “qu'vne machine soit tellement faite qu'elle profere des paroles […] Mais non pas qu'elle les arrange diuersement, pour respondre au sens de tout ce qui se dira en sa presence”

- **U02 — contests** (`pre-u02-descartes-1637-contests`; earliest-located; register nonfiction; 1637 (publication))
  - Descartes's Discours de la méthode (Leiden, 1637), Part V, says that machines made to resemble human bodies and imitate human actions could always be told from real people by two means, and that, even if they did some things as well as or better than people, they would act not by knowledge but only by the disposition of their organs.
  - Engagement: engages-premise — Gives reasons against a machine's thinking: speech that answers to the sense of whatever is said and action in all the occurrences of life need reason, a universal instrument, which a machine's particular dispositions of organs cannot supply.
  - Qualification: Stated of machines built to resemble human bodies and imitate human actions; the passage is part of Descartes's account of the difference between people and animals.   - Part V, pp. 56–57 (djvu pages 60–61), sentence beginning “Au lieu que s'il y en auoit” (supports): “s'il y en auoit qui eussent la ressemblance de nos cors, & imitassent autant nos actions que moralement il seroit possible, nous aurions tousiours deux moyens tres certains, pour reconnoistre qu'elles ne seroient point pour cela de vrais hommes.”
  - Part V, p. 57 (djvu page 61), the second means (supports): “bienqu'elles fissent plusieurs choses, aussy bien, ou peutestre mieux, qu'aucun de nous, elles manqueroient infalliblement en quelques autres, par lesquelles on découuriroit qu'elles n'agiroient pas par connoissance, mais seulement par la disposition de leurs organes”

### Turing, LMS lecture (1947)

`turing-1947-lms-lecture` · Lecture or talk · 1947-02-20 (delivery). As on the dataset entity and records: the date of the lecture in the archive's title.

- **U01 — contests** (`pre-u01-turing-1947-contests`; earliest-located; register nonfiction; 1947-02-20 (delivery))
  - Turing's lecture to the London Mathematical Society (20 February 1947) reports that it has been said that computing machines can only carry out the processes they are instructed to do, and argues that a machine whose instruction tables could modify themselves would be like a pupil who had added much by his own work.
  - Engagement: engages-premise — Grants the statement only for machines used as slaves on jobs thought out in detail, and argues that a machine able to modify its own instruction tables would make progress its makers had not foreseen.
  - Qualification: The lecture puts its case as a supposition (“Let us suppose we have set up a machine…”). The quoted report of the contested view names no one who held it. Proposed as U01's earliest located contesting text within this pack's searches.
  - Typescript p. 22 (scan 29), paragraph opening (context): “It has been said that computing machines can only carry out the processes that they are instructed to do.”
  - Typescript p. 22 (scan 29), same paragraph (supports): “It would be like a pupil who had learnt much from his master, but had added much more by his own work.”

- **U02 — articulates** (`pre-u02-turing-1947`; articulation; register nonfiction; 1947-02-20 (delivery))
  - Turing's lecture of 20 February 1947 says that when a machine with self-modifying instruction tables makes progress its makers had not foreseen, one is obliged to regard the machine as showing intelligence.
  - **Maintainer decision 12a (2026-11-03):** Listed under the U02 headline as the earliest located non-fiction text.
  - Qualification: The earliest located non-fiction text on U02 in this pack; Erewhon (1872, fiction) is earlier. The lecture also answers the argument from Gödel's theorem against machine intelligence (pp. 23–24).
  - Typescript p. 22 (scan 29) (supports): “When this happens I feel that one is obliged to regard the machine as showing intelligence.”
