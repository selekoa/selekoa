## Inputs and method

- **Records:** `research/prehistory/pack.json` (built from `drafts/*.json`; IDs begin `pre-`, which no dataset record uses). Admit from `pack.json`, not from the drafts, and carry the decided state: `pendingDecision`, `crossFamily`, `crossFamily2`, `maintainerDecision`, `maintainerDecision2`, `formerId`, `datasetLink`, `earliestLocatedFlag` and `hazardNote` are working fields; their content goes into the admission map, not into data. `checker-located.json` holds one withdrawn record under `withdrawn`; do not admit it.
- **Script:** `scripts/prehistory-admission.ts`, adapted from `scripts/heterodox-admission.ts` (same unit of record), with `--verify`, `--check` and `--base`. One script makes every data change. Map: `review/prehistory-admission-map-<date>.json`, every decision, correction, hold and field mapping with its reason. Before-snapshot `review/prehistory-admission-before-<date>.json`, first in the chain in `src/editorial/historical-views.ts`; update `transactions.test.ts` and `versions.test.ts`; cut the release with `pnpm release X`.
- **Verification at admission:** re-fetch the text sources and match each quotation mechanically (`tools.py merge` does the same; on 2026-11-04 every text source matched, 0 problems). Page-image sources (Descartes, the PubMed Central scan of Jefferson, the Turing typescripts, the NPL report, the Omni scan) cannot be matched mechanically; carry their "read on the page image" notes into the evidence notes, as earlier admissions did.
- **Editorial state:** every record is a seed. Machine agreement is not human review.

## The three new propositions

Concept entities, subtype "Proposition", with `proposition.code`, `criterion` and `terms` from [`propositions.json`](../research/prehistory/propositions.json). Entity descriptions follow the pattern of `machine-succession` ("A proposition about machine intelligence, recorded with one criterion so that texts can be classified against it (code U01 in the collection of antecedent texts). A record says what a text states, not whether the proposition is true.").

### U01 `machines-only-do-what-they-are-ordered`: A machine can only do what it is ordered to do

- **Criterion:** "A machine, whether a particular machine or machines in general, cannot originate anything: it does only what it has been ordered, instructed or programmed to do, so that it produces nothing beyond what was put into it or what the people who set it up know how to specify. A statement that machines are at present used only in this way, without saying that they could not do otherwise, does not meet the criterion; nor does a statement about one machine's lack of a particular skill."
- **Terms:** "Lovelace's objection", "Lady Lovelace's objection".
- **Records (10 admitted, 1 held):** `pre-u01-lovelace-1843-notes` (earliest located), `pre-u01-turing-1947-contests` (earliest located contesting), `pre-u01-turing-1948-npl-contests`, `pre-u01-hartree-1949`, `pre-u01-turing-1950-contests` (earliest located use of the name), `pre-u01-turing-c1951-contests`, `pre-u01-turing-1951-contests`, `pre-u01-samuel-1959-contests`, `pre-u01-wiener-1960-contests`, `pre-u01-wiener-1961-cybernetics-contests`. Held: `pre-u01-samuel-1960`.
- **Headlines:**
  - Earliest located: A. A. L. (attributed to Ada Lovelace), Notes to Menabrea's Sketch of the Analytical Engine (1843).
  - Earliest located text that contests it: Turing, lecture to the London Mathematical Society (1947-02-20).
  - Earliest located use of the name: Turing, Computing Machinery and Intelligence (1950-10), "Lady Lovelace's objection".

### U02 `machines-could-think`: A machine could think

- **Criterion:** "A machine could think, or could have intelligence, consciousness or a mind in the sense in which these are ascribed to people. The text may state it as a possibility in principle, a prediction or a programme of work, and may state it of machines generally or of a kind of machine. A statement that machines imitate or simulate particular mental operations, without the claim that this is or could amount to thinking, does not meet the criterion; nor does a figure of speech such as 'electronic brain' used without that claim."
- **Terms:** none.
- **Records (10):** `pre-u02-descartes-1637-contests` (earliest located contesting), `pre-u02-butler-1863-darwin-among-the-machines` (earliest located; satirical), `pre-u02-erewhon-1872` (fiction), `pre-u02-capek-rur-1923` (fiction; decision 7a; formerly `pre-u02-capek-rur-1923-context`), `pre-u02-turing-1947`, `pre-u02-jefferson-1949-contests`, `pre-u02-turing-1950` (context; decision 8a), `pre-u02-turing-c1951`, `pre-u02-turing-1951`, `pre-u02-kolmogorov-1961-letter` (1961 · wording attested 2023).
- **Headlines (decision 12a):**
  - Earliest located: Butler, Darwin among the Machines (1863-06-13) · Satirical.
  - Under it, two dated lines: earliest located novel, Butler, _Erewhon_ (1872); earliest located non-fiction text, Turing, lecture to the London Mathematical Society (1947-02-20). See "Interface follow-up".
  - Earliest located text that contests it: Descartes, _Discours de la méthode_ (1637).

### U03 `technological-singularity`: Machine intelligence will bring a singularity

- **Criterion (decision 3b, machine intelligence only):** "The creation of intelligence greater than human, by machines or by human intelligence amplified with machines, is approaching or would bring a point, called a singularity or described as a discontinuity or transition, beyond which human affairs as they have been known could not continue or could not be understood or foreseen. A statement that technological change in general is accelerating towards such a point, without machine intelligence as its cause or part of it, does not meet the criterion; nor does a prediction of superhuman machine intelligence without the claim of such a point."
- **Terms:** none (decision 3b assigns no name role).
- **Records (3, or 2 if Ulam is held):** `pre-u03-ulam-1958-context` (context; relay; conditional, see "Held"), `pre-u03-vinge-1983-first-word` (earliest located), `pre-u03-vinge-1993`.
- **Headlines:**
  - Earliest located: Vinge, First Word (Omni, 1983-01). Ulam's memoir of 1958 is shown as context.
  - Earliest located text that contests it: none. The searches (the pack's and the checker's) located no text disputing the singular transition itself; the checker's reading of Descartes (1637) as a qualified contest of one premise is in the search note, not recorded.

## Points to settle before admission

Two consequences of the decisions that the decision sheet did not show. Each has a recommendation; the data session applies it unless the maintainer answers otherwise.

1. **C1's criterion under 5a.** The checker accepted reading a only if C1 says so: "If that is intended, state it in C1 and use Descartes as context; do not claim this follows from the current wording alone." **Recommended:** add one sentence to C1's criterion, as the heterodox admission clarified W05's: "A text that holds that a machine would fail a behavioural test, and that the test therefore reliably tells machines from people, does not meet the criterion, even where it says that narrower performances would not show understanding." **Introduction (draft):**

> This collection records what texts written before 1965 say about whether machines can think or originate anything. Two propositions are stated and disputed in the located texts: that a machine can only do what it is ordered to do (U01), and that a machine could think (U02). The located texts run from Descartes's _Discours de la méthode_ (1637) to Kolmogorov's letter to A. I. Berg (1961). A third proposition, that machine intelligence will bring a singularity (U03), appears in the located texts only from 1983. Ulam's memoir of von Neumann (1958) reports an earlier conversation about technology in general, and is shown as context.
>
> The year 1965 is an editorial boundary. It was chosen because the dataset already holds I. J. Good's "Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine" (1965), which the superintelligence collection records. Nothing in the period itself marks that year, and several of Alan Turing's texts of the 1950s could have served as the anchor instead. The boundary may be revised.
>
> Two texts are fiction and one is a satirical letter. Such a record documents what the work says, not its author's view. Where a text survives only in a later copy, the date of its wording is shown beside the work's date.
>
> The same texts also bear on propositions in other collections, such as machine succession (W01) and behavioural tests as evidence (C1). Those records appear in their own collections; the links under each proposition lead to them.
